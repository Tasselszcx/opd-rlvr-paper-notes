RLxF: Reinforcement Learning from World Feedback

A Few Teacher Steps Go a Long Way: Cost-Efficient On-Policy Data Augmentation for Agent Post-Training

For LLM agents, supervised fine-tuning is not only about teacher labels’ quality, but also about which interaction contexts those labels condition on. Pure behavioral cloning uses full teacher demonstrations, creating a mismatch between teacher-induced contexts seen in training and student-induced contexts encountered at test time. Recent work addresses this mismatch by querying a teacher at contexts reached by the student, often with increasingly elaborate filtering of the teacher’s continuations. We instead frame on-policy data construction as a budget-allocation problem: under matched supervision resources, should teacher output be spent on more start-to-finish demos, longer continuations, outcome filtering, or broader coverage of learner-induced contexts? We formalize this design space through the rollout policy, switch-time distribution, continuation horizon, filtering rules, and two complementary costs: teacher inference generated before filtering and teacher supervision retained for SFT. Across HotpotQA, ALFWorld, and Terminal-Bench-Dev, bounded unfiltered teacher continuations at learner-induced contexts improve over pure behavioral cloning at matched budgets. On HotpotQA and ALFWorld, where we run the full comparison, few-step continuations match or exceed success-filtered and critical-context-filtered alternatives. Our findings suggest that a few teacher steps, placed at learner-induced contexts, can be a more cost-efficient supervision allocation than longer or more heavily curated teacher completions.

1 Introduction

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) trains a language-model agent by teacher forcing on labeled assistant turns ( Williams and Zipser, 1989 ) . In agent post-training, those labels are often generated by a capable but expensive teacher; the simplest dataset is then a corpus of end-to-end teacher demonstrations. We call this instantiation pure behavioral cloning, or pure BC for short. It is easy to implement, but inherits a familiar weakness from imitation learning: the student is trained on input contexts induced by the teacher, while at test time it acts on contexts induced by its own earlier decisions. For multi-turn agents, an early imperfect action changes the subsequent observation stream, after which the student may see contexts that are poorly represented in the teacher-demonstration corpus. This motivates DAgger-style interactive imitation learning ( Ross et al., 2011 ; Kelly et al., 2019 ) , and recent LLM-agent work has adapted the idea by querying a teacher at states reached by the student ( Choudhury and Sodhi, 2025 ; Lyu et al., 2025 ; Lauffer et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2026 ) .

Once one collects teacher supervision at learner-induced states, however, the central design question is not simply whether to use “on-policy” data. It is how to allocate a limited supervision budget. Additional teacher output could be spent on more end-to-end teacher demonstrations, longer teacher continuations after a student prefix, outcome-filtered continuations, or bounded corrections spread across more learner-induced states. These choices can all be described as adding supervised data, but their costs are not comparable under a generic unit such as “one training sample.” We therefore propose to track two concrete cost measures: $C_{\mathrm{i}}$ , the total teacher inference spent before filtering, and $C_{\mathrm{tr}}$ , the teacher-generated supervision retained and trained on per SFT epoch. This separates protocols that are cheap to generate from those that are cheap to train on after filtering.

A second comparability issue is that even the phrase “randomly sample a learner state” is ambiguous. For episodic LM agents, the relevant state is a learner-induced context generated by a causal hierarchy: first a task prompt is sampled, then a variable-length sequence of interaction turns unfolds, and within each turn the assistant tokens are generated autoregressively with strong serial dependence. Once rollout lengths vary, averaging over this process at the episode level or at the pooled-turn level no longer induces the same context distribution. We make these choices explicit through a unified data-construction framework specifying the rollout policy, switch-time distribution, continuation horizon, filtering rules, and induced context distribution. This framework’s goal is not to introduce a more complex algorithm, but to modularize design choices of on-policy data augmentation protocols.

We then study a stylized one-round augmentation setting. First, train an initial behavioral-cloning checkpoint on a teacher-demonstration corpus $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{BC}}$ . Next, roll out this student on training tasks, sample contexts from the learner-induced trajectories, and ask the teacher to continue from those contexts. Finally, reinitialize from the base student and train on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{BC}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{OP}}$ . We call this recipe BC+OP . Our main variant, OP-Short , keeps every sampled teacher continuation but caps it to a small number $K$ of teacher turns. The continuation length $K$ controls a breadth-depth tradeoff: a few teacher steps can show local recovery behavior after a student-induced prefix, but longer continuations spend more budget on teacher-descendant contexts rather than on covering additional learner-induced states. We compare OP-Short with Pure-BC , which spends the budget on additional full teacher demonstrations, and with filtered variants OP-Success and OP-Critical , which keep successful teacher continuations and, in the latter case, focus queries on tasks the student fails.

Across HotpotQA (question answering with search tool) ( Yang et al., 2018 ) , ALFWorld (text-based embodied planning) ( Shridhar et al., 2021 ) , and Terminal-Bench-Dev (command-line coding tasks) ( OpenThoughts-Agent Team, 2025 ) , bounded teacher continuations at learner-induced states are a consistently cost-effective form of SFT data augmentation. At matched supervision budgets, OP-Short improves over scaling Pure-BC . On HotpotQA, where we run the full comparison, unfiltered few-step continuations match or exceed the filtered alternatives depending on the cost axis. On ALFWorld and Terminal-Bench-Dev, the best continuation length is larger than in HotpotQA, but additional teacher turns still do not monotonically improve performance. On Terminal-Bench-Dev, OP-Short with a small fraction of the SFT corpus and no reinforcement-learning stage matches the OpenThoughts-Agent baseline that uses the full corpus followed by reinforcement learning.

Our results suggest a practical rule for agent SFT under constrained expert supervision budgets: use pure teacher demonstrations to obtain a reasonable initial student, then allocate additional budget to bounded teacher continuations at learner-induced contexts before investing in full rollouts or heavier filtering. The right continuation length is domain-dependent, but across our experiments a few teacher steps often go a long way. Our contributions are:

We formulate SFT data construction for agent post-training as a budget-allocation problem, separating teacher-inference cost $C_{\mathrm{i}}$ from retained SFT training cost $C_{\mathrm{tr}}$ .

We provide a formal framework for describing on-policy data augmentation that makes explicit the context-generation policy, switch-time distribution, continuation horizon, filtering rules, and how they affect the induced context distribution in variable-length rollouts.

We empirically compare bounded unfiltered teacher continuations, filtered continuations, critical-context filtering, and pure behavioral cloning across three agentic benchmarks, finding that a few on-policy teacher steps are often the most cost-effective use of additional teacher supervision.

2 Methods

2.1 Modeling Agentic Interactions as a POMDP

Our experiments involve ReAct-style agents ( Yao et al., 2022 ) , each parametrized by an LLM policy $\pi\in\Pi$ . Such an agent interacts with a digital environment by generating assistant turns that optionally contain tool call(s) and receiving environment responses in ChatML format. 1 1 1 Chat Markup Language (ChatML; ( OpenAI, 2023 ) ) defines a structured role-annotated LLM context formatting template (e.g. system , user , assistant roles), including serialization protocol. It is widely adopted by open-weight LLMs too. An agent’s goal is to maximize terminally verifiable rewards on a domain-specific collection of tasks $\mathcal{Q}$ , split into a training set $\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{train}}$ and a test set $\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{test}}$ for performance reporting. Specifying the task collection naturally includes a collection of environments and reward functions. We model the aggregate domain-level dynamics as a finite-horizon Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP, ( Smallwood and Sondik, 1973 ; Kaelbling et al., 1998 ) ), denoting it by a tuple $\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{O},\mathrm{P_{s}},\mathrm{P_{o}},\mu,r\,|\,T_{max},\mathcal{H}\rangle$ . In agentic settings, a task $q\in\mathcal{Q}$ is often identified by its initial state, transition and observation kernels, and reward function; to simplify notation, we mostly suppress this $q$ -dependence below.

$\mathcal{S}$ is the latent environment state space. $\mathcal{A}$ , or action set, is the text space of producible assistant turns. The observation space $\mathcal{O}$ is the text space of one-step environment responses returned by the digital environment and mediated by an agent scaffold. 2 2 2 Also referred to as an “agent harness” in the AI agents literature. See for example ( Anthropic, 2026 ) . At turn $t>0$ , a full assistant turn (a token sequence ending at a pre-programmed stop rule) is sampled from the LLM policy,

 | $a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid h_{t-1}),$ |  | (1)

where $h_{t-1}\in\mathcal{H}$ is the LLM-readable context we define later. The environment then evolves according to the transition kernel while the agent scaffold extracts the environment response:

[TABLE S2.E2]
 | $s_{t}\sim\mathrm{P_{s}}(\cdot\mid s_{t-1},a_{t}),\qquad o_{t}\sim\mathrm{P_{o}}(\cdot\mid s_{t}).$ |  | (2)

This distributional notation also covers the special case of deterministic digital settings where $\mathrm{P_{s}}$ and/or $\mathrm{P_{o}}$ are Dirac delta kernels. A rollout terminates at the stopping time $T\leq T_{max}$ , either when the assistant turn indicates task completion or when the horizon is reached. We set $o_{T}\equiv\varnothing$ .

Let $\mu$ be the empirical distribution over tasks $q\in\mathcal{Q}$ . Drawing $q\sim\mu$ fixes that task’s initial environment state $s_{0}$ , its kernels $(P_{s},P_{o})$ , and its reward function $r$ , so a distribution over tasks and a distribution over $s_{0}$ carry the same information in our setting; we write $q\sim\mu$ throughout. In the basic setting $\mu$ is uniform over the task collection under study, and $\mu_{\mathrm{train}},\mu_{\mathrm{test}}$ denote its restrictions to $\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{train}},\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{test}}$ . Reward is terminal: the reward function $r:\mathcal{S}\to[0,1]$ is read only at the terminal environment state, yielding the realized return $r:=r(s_{T})$ with no intermediate reward. This is the RLVR setting, where the environment returns a single verifiable outcome at termination.

A trajectory ( $\tau$ ) is a turn-level, complete-information representation of an agent’s interaction and environment history in a single episode. For a fixed task $q$ and policy $\pi$ ,

[TABLE S2.E3]
 | $\tau_{0}=(q,s_{0}),\;\;\tau_{t}=(q,s_{0},a_{1},s_{1},o_{1},\ldots,a_{t},s_{t},o_{t}),\;\;\tau=(q,s_{0},\ldots,a_{T},s_{T},o_{T},r),$ |  | (3)

where $T$ is the terminal turn (i.e. horizon). As an aside, we reserve the word “rollout” for the sampling procedure that runs a policy in the environment and realizes a trajectory. Let $\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{q}$ denote $\tau$ ’s probability distribution given a task $q$ . Given a task distribution $\mu$ ,

[TABLE S2.E4]
 | $\mathbb{P}_{\mu}^{\pi}(\tau):=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\mu}\left[\mathbb{P}_{q}^{\pi}(\tau)\right].$ |  | (4)

During the process of rolling out $\tau$ , the partial trajectory $\tau_{t}$ at each turn $t<T$ is projected by the agent scaffold to the context state $h_{t}\in\mathcal{H}$ (“context”). We call such a mapping $H$ and define:

[TABLE S2.E5]
 | $h_{0}=H(\tau_{0}):=(q),\quad h_{t}=H(\tau_{t}):=h_{t-1}+(a_{t},o_{t})=(q,a_{1},o_{1},\ldots,a_{t},o_{t}).$ |  | (5)

In addition, given a completed trajectory $\tau$ with terminal length $T$ , we also denote the retrospective construction of $h_{t}$ by $H_{t}(\tau)$ , which equals $H(\tau_{t})$ when $t\leq T$ , and $\mathrm{N/A}\notin\mathcal{H}$ when $t>T$ .

Note that a context $h_{t}$ ’s serialized form is the information state the LLM policy $\pi$ conditions its action on at time $t$ (Eq. 1 ), not the latent environment state $s_{t}$ . Therefore, we use the unmodified “state” to alias context hereafter, referring to “state” and “context” interchangeably. 2.2 Context Distribution and the SFT Objective

In the original DAgger paper ( Ross et al., 2011 ) , the trajectory stopping time was assumed to be a constant $T$ , and they instantiated the state distribution (a.k.a. state occupancy measure in the RL theory literature) as a time-uniform distribution $d^{\pi}(s)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d^{\pi}_{t}(s)$ , where they hid the $\mu$ -dependence and described $d^{\pi}_{t}$ as “ the distribution of states at time $t$ if the learner executed policy $\pi$ from step $1$ to $t-1$ . ” We observe that using such a notation to describe episodes with varying lengths, such as in agentic LLM tasks, is under-specified: when $T$ is random, how could we instantiate the Monte Carlo integration of $\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}d_{t}^{\pi}]$ ? We try to formalize the inductive biases a practitioner can choose to resolve the “average over what” question.

Recall from ( 4 ) and ( 5 ) that $\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mu}$ is the trajectory distribution marginalized over $\mu$ , and that given a finished trajectory $\tau$ , $H_{t}(\tau)\in\mathcal{H}\cup\{\mathrm{N/A}\}$ is the retrospectively constructed context. Define the time- $t$ marginal context distribution $h_{t}\sim d^{\pi}_{\mu,t}$ , which implicitly assumes that $T(\tau)\geq t$ for the $\tau$ that emits $h_{t}$ , as a trajectory-average counting measure:

[TABLE S2.E6]
 | $d_{\mu,t}^{\pi}(B):=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mu}^{\pi}}\!\left[\,\mathbf{1}\{H_{t-1}(\tau)\in B\}\,\right],\quad\text{for any set of contexts }B\subseteq\mathcal{H}.$ |  | (6)

We now introduce a general, any-time context distribution $d_{\mu}^{\pi;w}$ , s.t. $h\sim d_{\mu}^{\pi;w}$ where $h$ ’s turn count $t(h)$ is jointly sampled. As the POMDP analogue of the state distribution, it is parametrizable by $\mu$ , $\pi$ and a turn-indexed context weighting function $w_{t}(\tau)\geq 0$ ( $w$ for short, when unambiguous):

[TABLE S2.E7]
 | $d_{\mu}^{\pi;w}(B)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mu}^{\pi}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T(\tau)}\left(w_{t}(\tau)\,\cdot\,\mathbf{1}\left\{H_{t-1}(\tau)\in B\right\}\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mu}^{\pi}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T(\tau)}w_{t}(\tau)\right]},\quad\text{for any set of contexts }B\subseteq\mathcal{H}.$ |  | (7)

The choice of $w_{t}$ lets Eq. 7 distinguish two state averaging conventions that only coincide when $T(\tau)$ is constant with probability $1$ for $\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mu}^{\pi}$ :

[TABLE S2.E8]
 | $\displaystyle w_{t}(\tau)=1\;\;\longrightarrow\;\;d_{\mu}^{\pi;\mathbf{1}}(B)$ | $\displaystyle:=d_{\mu}^{\pi;\mathbf{pool}}(B)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T(\tau)}\mathbf{1}\left\{H_{t-1}(\tau)\in B\right\}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[T(\tau)]},$ |  | (8)
 | $\displaystyle w_{t}(\tau)=\frac{1}{T(\tau)}\;\;\longrightarrow\;\;d_{\mu}^{\pi;\mathbf{\frac{1}{T}}}(B)$ | $\displaystyle:=d_{\mu}^{\pi;\mathbf{traj}}(B)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\!\left[\frac{1}{T(\tau)}\sum_{t=1}^{T(\tau)}\mathbf{1}\left\{H_{t-1}(\tau)\in B\right\}\right].$ | 

Figure 1: The two state-averaging conventions of Eq. 8 , on
two sampled trajectories of (a) identical and (b) variable length.

The first is pooled-turns randomization: every visited context, regardless of its trajectory, receives equal probability mass after normalization, so longer trajectories contribute more total mass. A fixed-horizon switch sampler with invalid-switch rejection, as in OEC-style random switching, is one implementation that induces this convention; Appendix B.2 gives the derivation. The second is trajectory-uniform randomization: roll out $\tau$ until termination, then sample a turn $t\sim\text{Unif}\{1,T(\tau)\}$ . In this case, every trajectory (episode) receives equal probability mass. The latter is the randomization induced by our uniform-prefix takeover rule used in § 3.1 and § 3.2 .

We now define the SFT objective independently of how the context distribution is induced. Let $\pi_{e}\in\Pi^{\star}$ be a frozen teacher policy and $\pi_{\theta}\in\Pi^{\mathrm{\Theta}}$ be the student policy being fine-tuned. For any turn-level context distribution $d$ over $\mathcal{H}$ , define the next-turn population SFT objective as the student policy minimizing the forward KL divergence averaged on $d$ :

[TABLE S2.E9]
 | $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta;d):=\mathbb{E}_{h\sim d}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{e}(\cdot\mid h)\,\middle\|\,\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h)\right)\right].$ |  | (9)

Since $\pi_{e}$ is fixed, minimizing Eq. 9 is the same as minimizing the $d$ -averaged cross entropy

[TABLE S2.E10]
 | $\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta;d):=\mathbb{E}_{h\sim d}\left(\mathbb{E}_{a^{\star}\sim\pi_{e}(\cdot\mid h)}\left[-\log\pi_{\theta}(a^{\star}\mid h)\right]\right)$ |  | (10)

up to an additive constant independent of $\theta$ . Here $a^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}$ is a complete teacher-generated assistant turn, matching our choice of the SFT objective to be next-turn-level: on an interaction context $h$ , the student is trained to maximize the joint likelihood of teacher’s next assistant turn’s tokens. The single-teacher-turn assumption of Eq. 10 can be easily extended for the case of continuing multiple teacher turns by autoregressive factorization. Appendix B.1 formalizes this multi-turn extension.

$d$ ’s parametrization distinguishes supervision regimes. Standard pure behavioral cloning SFT corresponds to a teacher-induced and pooled-turn context distribution $d=d_{\mu}^{\pi_{e};\mathbf{pool}}$ , i.e. $d=d_{\mu}^{\pi_{e};w}$ with $w\equiv 1$ . A more “on-policy” supervision scheme, we denote by $d=d_{\mu}^{\rho;w}$ , would instead use a context-generating policy $\rho\neq\pi_{e}$ ; $\rho$ can be a student $\pi_{\theta}$ , a teacher-student mixture, or a more complex composition. $w_{t}(\tau)$ offers another flexible knob to configure $d$ : besides the two $t$ -agnostic instances in Eq. 8 , it may be beneficial for future work to explore $w_{t}(\tau)$ as a per-context-turn value function. Lastly, $\mu$ may be instantiated as the empirical distribution over a finite task collection.

2.3 The Budget Allocation Problem in Fine-Tuning Dataset Construction

Separate from the sequential decision process of an LLM’s rollout, researchers face a different decision problem when post-training it for downstream applications: how to allocate a given fine-tuning resource budget to maximize final performance on the test set. In this paper, the teacher is an advanced LLM whose model size and inference cost are much larger than the student’s, but it can be instantiated as a human expert too. We quantify the budget constraint in two separate axes:

$C_{\mathrm{i}}$ : Total teacher inference cost , before custom data filtering

$C_{\mathrm{tr}}$ : Per-epoch training cost , i.e. GPU hours needed on the (filtered) SFT traces

Both $C_{\mathrm{i}}$ and $C_{\mathrm{tr}}$ are measurable either in tokens or the number assistant turns generated by the teacher. For $C_{\mathrm{tr}}$ , the GPU hours needed by an SFT training run is roughly proportional to the number of accepted teacher tokens on an epoch-normalized basis. Whether the spending on $C_{\mathrm{i}}$ or $C_{\mathrm{tr}}$ dominates a research team’s fine-tuning budget concern, we suppose, is often idiosyncratic. For example, a research group with a dedicated allocation on a large GPU cluster faces near-zero marginal training cost, so they would be far more cost-sensitive to external API inference calls ( $C_{\mathrm{i}}$ ) needed to generate teacher completions than to the size of the resulting training corpus ( $C_{\mathrm{tr}}$ ). So rather than assigning an ad-hoc weight, our experiments visualize budget scaling under each extreme point respectively.

Beyond inference and training compute, task diversity in $\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{train}}$ , which we proxy by $|\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{train}}|$ , also shapes a student’s learning efficiency; recall from § 2.1 that $(\mu,\mathrm{P_{s}},\mathrm{P_{o}},r)$ is often implemented as an empirical distribution over $\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{train}}$ . To avoid confounding effect from this factor, we generally control $|\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{train}}|$ when comparing among OP and pure BC variants, so differences along the cost axes isolate the effect of the data-construction recipe alone. Any variation in $|\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{train}}|$ is explicitly indicated in a plot. We treat $|\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{train}}|$ as a hyperparameter rather than a third budget axis since it is often much more expensive and inelastic than $C_{\mathrm{i}}$ and $C_{\mathrm{tr}}$ : the pool of domain-specific training tasks with verifiable answers is typically determined by whatever open-source or proprietary datasets are available at the fine-tuning phase’s onset, and meaningfully expanding its coverage requires highly costly human annotation effort that is qualitatively different from scaling teacher inference or GPU compute.

2.4 Design Space of Fine-Tuning Dataset Construction

We present a common recipe to connect implementation-level data curation knobs to the population SFT objective in Eq. 10 . In code, these knobs are easily mutable objects compatible with LLM post-training libraries like verl or LlamaFactory : the policy checkpoint generating prefix contexts, the switch-time sampler, the prefix-context and continuation filters, the teacher-continuation horizon, etc. Connecting the formalism in § 2.1 -§ 2.3 , choosing these objects instantiates the empirical context distribution corresponding to a population distribution $d$ and to deciding how much teacher inference $C_{\mathrm{i}}$ and accepted training signal $C_{\mathrm{tr}}$ are spent to approximate it.

Let $\rho$ be the prefix-context policy and $\pi_{e}$ the teacher labeler. A general implementation recipe is

[TABLE S2.E11]
 | $\mathcal{G}:=(\rho,\pi_{e},n,\Psi,\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{switch}},K,\Phi),$ |  | (11)

where $n$ is the number of SFT context-continuation traces proposed per task, $\Psi$ is a prefix-context filter applied before teacher continuation, $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{switch}}$ is a protocol to sample a candidate switch time $t^{\prime}$ , $K$ caps the number of teacher assistant turns in the continuation, and $\Phi$ is a post-continuation filter. The realized count of accepted traces $N$ is therefore random and can be smaller than $n\cdot|\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{train}}|$ . For each trace proposal on task $q$ , first the context-generating policy is rolled out to produce a trajectory $\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{q}^{\rho}$ and either $t^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{switch}}(\tau)$ or $t^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{switch}}$ independently of $\tau$ .

If $t^{\prime}\leq T(\tau)$ and $\Psi(\tau,t^{\prime})=1$ , the proposal is explored further: the (partial) trajectory up to the $t^{\prime}$ -th turn induces the prefix context $h_{t^{\prime}-1}=H(\tau_{t^{\prime}-1})$ . The teacher continues the interaction from $H(\tau_{t^{\prime}-1})$ and its associated state $s_{t^{\prime}-1}$ for $k^{\prime}\leq K$ assistant turns before finish. We denote the aggregate trajectory including the continuation, which may be rejected by the filter $\Phi$ , by

[TABLE S2.E12]
 | $\widetilde{\tau}=(q,s_{0},a_{1},s_{1},o_{1},\ldots,a_{t^{\prime}-1},s_{t^{\prime}-1},o_{t^{\prime}-1},a^{\star}_{t^{\prime}},s_{t^{\prime}},o_{t^{\prime}},\ldots,a^{\star}_{t^{\prime}+k^{\prime}-1}),$ |  | (12)

where starred actions are teacher-generated. Note that under the linear-context, no-format-change convention of Eq. 5 , $H(\tau_{t^{\prime}-1})=H(\widetilde{\tau}_{t^{\prime}-1})$ . If the teacher and student scaffolds use different chat templates and tokenizers, the teacher will see $H(\tau_{t^{\prime}-1})$ serialized in its own format, though the accepted continuation would be rendered in the student’s format before SFT. Altogether, the post-filter SFT dataset is the collection of context-continuation traces with superscript $i$ as index:

[TABLE S2.E13]
 | $\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{G}}:=\{(H_{t^{\prime}_{i}-1}(\tau^{i}),\,H(\widetilde{\tau}^{i})[t^{\prime}_{i}:]):i=1,\ldots,N\},$ |  | (13)

where $[t^{\prime}_{i}:]$ is an array slicing notation for the teacher-generated continuation segment. The SFT loss is computed only on teacher-generated assistant turns ( $a^{\star}_{\cdot}$ ) in the continuation, excluding environment observations. Eq. 13 cleanly expresses the intended separation: $\rho$ supplies the prefix context and $\pi_{e}$ supplies the continuation. Algorithm 1 summarizes the overall procedure of constructing $\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{G}}$ .

The way $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{switch}}$ samples $t^{\prime}$ implicitly defines the context weighting function $w$ in Eq. 7 and 8 . If $\tau$ is first completed and then $t^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{1,\ldots,T(\tau)\}$ , the induced context distribution is trajectory-uniform, $d^{\rho;\mathrm{traj}}_{\mu}$ and $w\equiv 1/T(\tau)$ . If instead $t^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{1,\ldots,T_{max}\}$ , i.e. a fixed support, and the pair $(\tau,t^{\prime})$ is rejected when $t^{\prime}>T(\tau)$ , then the accepted contexts follow the pooled-turn distribution $d^{\rho;\mathrm{pool}}_{\mu}$ with $w\equiv 1$ ; in other words, tasks and rollouts with shorter horizons naturally are less represented in accepted examples. Lauffer et al. (2025) ’s implementation resembles $d^{\rho;\mathrm{pool}}_{\mu}$ : it samples a switch index before the rollout, lets the student act until either the switch is reached or the student terminates, and discards trajectories that terminate before the teacher takes over (§ B.2 ).

When $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{switch}}\equiv 1$ , $(\rho,K,n)=(\pi_{e},T_{max},1)$ , and no filters, we call $\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{G}}$ “pure BC” and alias by $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{BC}}$ . [TABLE S2.E14]

OP-Critical adds the prefix-context filter $\Psi(\tau,t^{\prime})=\mathds{1}\{r(\tau)=0\}$ in our experiments, so teacher budget is spent only on tasks the rollout policy $\rho$ fails to solve on its own.

3 Experiments

We test the cost-and-performance framing of § 2 on three distinct agentic environments. On HotpotQA ( Yang et al., 2018 ) (search-based question answering) we compare all four recipe types in Table 1 , including varying $K$ for OP-Short and $n$ for Pure-BC . On ALFWorld ( Shridhar et al., 2021 ) (embodied household planning in text environments) we compare OP-Short at $K=3$ to pure BC at four training-instance budgets, with in-distribution and out-of-distribution evaluation, an ablation on the ratio of on-policy to expert-prefix data. On Terminal-Bench-Dev ( OpenThoughts-Agent Team, 2025 ) (solving code problems in an interactive shell) we sweep pure BC across five fractions of the BC corpus and run OP-Short at two continuation budgets across three prefix policies, and compare with the OpenThoughts-Agent baseline. For the three OP types in all our experiments, $\rho$ is $\pi_{1}$ from Eq. 14 , and the final augmented SFT set is $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{BC}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{OP}}$ (the same $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{BC}}$ used to obtain $\pi_{1}$ ).

3.1 Search Agent: HotpotQA

HotpotQA ( Yang et al., 2018 ) was originally designed to test a model’s ability to answer a question by synthesizing evidence from preset Wikipedia snippets. We use the Search-R1 + VeRL adaptation ( Jin et al., 2025 ; Sheng et al., 2025 ) , which removes the preset snippets and turns each question (i.e. task) into a sequential interaction with a searchable database: the agent interleaves thinking with search queries to a 2018 snapshot of the English Wikipedia, receives retrieved passages as observations, and terminates by submitting an answer in no more than $T_{\mathrm{max}}=5$ turns. Since this database is static, the environment state $s_{t}$ is time-invariant; the rollout’s meaningful evolution is the LLM-readable context $h_{t}$ as tool calls and observations accumulate. On-policy supervisions improve SFT sample efficiency. On both cost axes in Figure 2 , teacher completions from $\pi_{1}$ -induced prefixes make better use of the fixed question set than additional Pure-BC demonstrations. Pure-BC improves as more complete teacher traces are added but enters a region of diminishing returns, whereas the OP recipes achieve higher pass@1 at comparable supervision budgets, suggesting that once naive scaling of expert demonstrations saturates, redirecting teacher supervision toward student-induced contexts offers a higher marginal return per token spent.

Scaling teacher continuation length has non-monotone returns. The OP-Short $K$ -sweep shows that longer teacher continuations are not automatically better, even before cost normalization. Increasing $K$ helps up to $K=3$ , but $K=5$ is worse despite spending more teacher tokens. This non-monotonicity likely reflects two factors: the main learning is the bounded repair at the student-induced state, beyond which extra turns produce completions unlikely for a student; and in HotpotQA, trajectories that exhaust all $T_{\mathrm{max}}$ might correlate with more ill-posed questions, biasing long continuations to be less instructional.

Filtering trades teacher inference for fewer training tokens. The filtered recipes, OP-Success and OP-Critical , can look attractive on retained training cost $C_{\mathrm{tr}}$ because rejected continuations are not trained on. They are less favorable on teacher-inference cost $C_{\mathrm{i}}$ , where rejected teacher tokens are still charged. The gap between the two panels therefore clarifies the tradeoff: filtering is most useful when the SFT training compute is scarce, not when teacher inference is the binding resource.

4 Discussion

The main practical lesson is not that “on-policy” data should replace pure behavioral cloning. Rather, it is useful as targeted augmentation once BC has produced a student whose mistakes define informative contexts. In this view, the key knob is how much teacher-descendant trajectory to buy at each learner-induced context state. Our results suggest that bounded continuations often provide enough local recovery signal, while spending the same budget on longer completions, additional full demonstrations, or success filtering can reduce coverage of distinct student states. The continuation budget $K$ should therefore be treated as a domain-dependent breadth–depth parameter, not a universal constant. Filtering should likewise be chosen according to the binding cost axis: it can reduce retained SFT tokens, but rejected continuations still consume teacher inference.

Limitations.

We study one-round augmentation on three verifiable agentic benchmarks, with the most complete variant comparison on HotpotQA and ALFWorld. The conclusions may change for long-horizon tasks where recovery requires extended planning, for settings without automatic reward verification, or under iterative data collection. We also do not provide a theory predicting the optimal $K$ or the observed non-monotone returns to continuation length.

Appendix A Related Work

DAgger and on-policy SFT.

Our work builds on interactive imitation learning, especially DAgger [ Ross and Bagnell, 2010 , Ross et al., 2011 , Ross and Bagnell, 2014 , Kelly et al., 2019 ] , which reduces compounding error by
collecting expert labels at learner-induced states. Caccia et al. [2024] show that, for LLM agents, better prediction on expert trajectories need
not translate to better downstream performance once the student acts on
its own induced contexts, and mention DAgger as a potential remedy. Lauffer et al. [2025] are the closest prior
work to our intervention-style setup: they roll in with the student to a
random switch step, let the expert continue to termination, mask the
student prefix during training, and filter by verifiable success and
student repetition. This motivates our filtered baselines, whereas our
main OP-SHORT method keeps every sampled continuation but bounds it to
a few teacher turns. Other variants use privileged train-time teacher
state [ Choudhury and Sodhi, 2025 ] , teacher-identified first-error
corrections [ Lyu et al., 2025 ] , self-reflected teacher-guided
corrections [ Chen et al., 2025a ] , or turn-level teacher-student mixing
for long-horizon software agents [ Li et al., 2026 ] . Related methods
also address trajectory-level error accumulation through step-level
comparison [ Wang et al., 2025 ] , teacher-free first-error revision [ Yuan et al., 2025 ] , or off-policy selection of critical trajectory
segments [ Chen et al., 2025b , Lan et al., 2025 ] .

On-policy distillation.

A related line studies on-policy distillation (OPD) for autoregressive
language models, training on student-sampled sequences while matching
teacher behavior via generalized knowledge distillation [ Agarwal et al., 2024 ] , reverse-KL objectives [ Gu et al., 2024 ] ,
context internalization [ Ye et al., 2026 ] , privileged self-distillation [ Zhao et al., 2026b ] , verbal-score supervision [ Xiong et al., 2026 ] , or
black-box discriminator-based distillation [ Ye et al., 2025 ] .
Concurrently, Zhao et al. [2026a] decouple prefix source from KL
direction, identifying student-prefix forward-KL as DAgger-style
on-policy SFT and student-prefix reverse-KL as OPD. Our setting focuses
on explicit teacher completions at executable, learner-visited agent
states; this is natural for multi-turn tool-use, supports black-box
teachers, and lets us measure the budget in generated and retained
teacher tokens rather than in token-level distribution queries.

Rejection-sampling SFT and the SFT-as-RL connection.

A complementary line analyzes outcome-filtered SFT in its own right. Qin and Springenberg [2025] show that SFT on success-filtered trajectories
optimizes a lower bound on the sparse-reward RL objective and propose an
importance-weighted variant that tightens this bound. This perspective
varies which generated trajectories are retained; we additionally vary
the state distribution by querying the teacher at learner-induced prefix
states. Accordingly, we treat filtered SFT as one data-construction
choice within a broader budget-allocation problem, rather than only as a
stand-alone alternative to RL.

Positioning relative to prior work.

Recent agent post-training recipes often bundle several choices: prefix
policy, switch-time sampling, continuation length, masking, filtering,
and sometimes a subsequent RL stage. We instead make these choices
explicit and compare them under matched supervision resources, separating
teacher-inference cost from retained SFT training cost. This isolates the
question central to our experiments: once a reasonable behavioral-cloning
student is available, whether additional teacher budget is better spent
on more start-to-end expert demonstrations, longer or filtered teacher
continuations, or short unfiltered corrections spread over more
learner-induced contexts.

Appendix B Mathematical Details

B.1 Multi-turn teacher continuations and the SFT objective

§ 2.2 defines the population SFT objective at the single-next-turn level. We now spell out how the same objective can be revised to model SFT on multi-turn continuations for § 2.4 recipes with $K>1$ .

One notational distinction is useful before defining the multi-turn objective. In the single-next-turn objective of Eq. 10 , a sample from $d$ only needs to specify an LLM-readable context $h$ , since the teacher action is used as a supervised label and need not be executed. For a multi-turn teacher continuation, however, the teacher’s first action must be executed to produce the next observation, so the sampled object must also contain a replayable environment state. We therefore let $\bar{d}$ denote a distribution over executable partial trajectories , which include replicable environment snapshots, whose context pushforward recovers the main-text context distribution $d$ :

[TABLE A2.E15]
 | $H_{\#}\bar{d}=d,\qquad\tau_{t-1}\sim\bar{d},\qquad h_{t-1}=H(\tau_{t-1}).$ |  | (15)

In our digital agent environments, such as HotpotQA, ALFWorld, Terminal-Bench, and SWE-Bench-style settings, this replayable prefix is typically stored or reconstructed losslessly from the task seed, scaffold state, and action history; this is much easier to re-simulate than in physical environments, where replaying a trajectory may introduce non-trivial sensor and environment variation.

Consider a partial trajectory $\tau_{t-1}$ with LLM-readable context $h_{t-1}=H(\tau_{t-1})$ . A teacher continuation from this context consists of at most $K$ subsequent teacher turns and environment observations,

[TABLE A2.E16]
 | $y_{\geq t}=(a_{t}^{\star},o_{t},\ldots,a_{t+L-1}^{\star},o_{t+L-1}),\qquad L\leq K,$ |  | (16)

where $L$ is a random variable that is completely determined by $y_{\geq t}$ ’s realization and may be much smaller than $K$ if the episode terminates early. We write

[TABLE A2.E17]
 | $y_{\geq t}\sim C_{e}^{K}(\cdot\mid\tau_{t-1})$ |  | (17)

for the distribution over such continuations obtained by repeatedly sampling teacher actions and stepping the environment:

[TABLE A2.E18]
 | $a_{t+j}^{\star}\sim\pi_{e}(\cdot\mid h_{t+j-1}),\qquad s_{t+j}\sim\mathrm{P}_{s}(\cdot\mid s_{t+j-1},a_{t+j}^{\star}),\qquad o_{t+j}\sim\mathrm{P}_{o}(\cdot\mid s_{t+j}),$ |  | (18)

for $j=0,\ldots,L-1$ , and the context extended as

[TABLE A2.E19]
 | $h_{t+j}=H(\tau_{t+j})=h_{t+j-1}+(a_{t+j}^{\star},o_{t+j}).$ |  | (19)

Thus $C_{e}^{K}$ is the closed-loop continuation operator induced by the teacher policy, the environment dynamics, the observation kernel, and the stopping rule. A completion filter $\Phi$ re-weights this continuation law by rejecting some continuations. We denote the accepted traces’ law by $C_{e}^{K,\Phi}$ .

From the student policy’s perspective, the likelihood of a continuation $y_{\geq t}$ factors sequentially as

[TABLE A2.E20]
 | $p_{\theta}(y_{\geq t}\mid\tau_{t-1})=\prod_{j=0}^{L-1}\pi_{\theta}(a_{t+j}^{\star}\mid h_{t+j-1})\cdot\mathrm{P}_{s}(s_{t+j}\mid s_{t+j-1},a_{t+j}^{\star})\cdot\mathrm{P}_{o}(o_{t+j}\mid s_{t+j}).$ |  | (20)

The environment transition and observation terms do not depend on $\theta$ . Therefore, up to an additive constant independent of $\theta$ , the negative log-likelihood of an accepted continuation is

[TABLE A2.E21]
 | $\ell_{\mathrm{sft}}(\theta;\tau_{t-1},y_{\geq t})=\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}-\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t+j}^{\star}\mid h_{t+j-1}).$ |  | (21)

Equivalently, the multi-turn population SFT objective induced by a replayable prefix distribution $\bar{d}$ and an accepted teacher-continuation law is

[TABLE A2.E22]
 | $\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{sft}}^{K,\Phi}(\theta;\bar{d})=\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{t-1}\sim\bar{d}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y_{\geq t}\sim C_{e}^{K,\Phi}(\cdot\mid\tau_{t-1})}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{L(y_{\geq t})-1}-\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t+j}^{\star}\mid h_{t+j-1})\right]\right].$ |  | (22)

This expression should be read at the turn level: each $a_{t+j}^{\star}$ is a complete assistant turn, whose cross entropy is implemented as a sum over its per-token log probabilities. The summation over $j$ does not assume that the teacher turns within $y_{\geq t}$ are independent. Rather, it is the sequential factorization of the likelihood under teacher forcing, where each later context is obtained by appending the earlier teacher action and environment observation.

For $K=1$ , only the pushforward context distribution $d=H_{\#}\bar{d}$ matters and this reduces to the next-turn objective in Eq. 10 . For $K>1$ , the first loss term supervises the student at the sampled context $h_{t-1}$ , while later terms supervise the student at teacher-descendant contexts induced by the same continuation. Thus increasing $K$ trades broader coverage of sampled contexts for deeper teacher recovery from each sampled context.

B.2 $\mathrm{Unif}\{1,\ldots,T_{max}\}$ switching with invalid-switch rejection induces pooled-turn sampling

This appendix justifies the pooled-turn statement for the proposal-budget convention used in Algorithm 1 . Consider one switch proposal. Sample a task $q\sim\mu$ , roll out the context-generating policy to obtain $\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{q}^{\rho}$ with stopping time $T(\tau)\leq T_{\mathrm{max}}$ , and independently sample $U\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{1,\ldots,T_{\mathrm{max}}\}$ . Accept the proposal iff $U\leq T(\tau)$ ; otherwise no prefix context exists at that turn and the proposal is discarded. For any measurable set of contexts $B\subseteq\mathcal{H}$ ,

[TABLE A2.EGx1]
 | $\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{H_{U-1}(\tau)\in B\mid U\leq T(\tau)\}$ | $\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\mu,\,\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{q}^{\rho}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T(\tau)}\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathbf{1}\{H_{t-1}(\tau)\in B\}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\mu,\,\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{q}^{\rho}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T(\tau)}\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{max}}}\right]}$ |  | (23)
 |  | $\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mu}^{\rho}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T(\tau)}\mathbf{1}\{H_{t-1}(\tau)\in B\}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mu}^{\rho}}[T(\tau)]}=d^{\rho;\mathrm{pool}}_{\mu}(B).$ |  | (24)

Thus sampling a global-horizon switch and rejecting switches that occur after the realized trajectory terminates induces the same accepted prefix-context distribution as the pooled-turn convention of Eq. 8 . With an empirical task set and $n$ proposal attempts per task, the same calculation applies with $\mu$ equal to the uniform empirical distribution over $\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{train}}$ . Tasks whose realized rollouts are shorter simply produce fewer accepted proposals in expectation, since $\mathbb{P}(U\leq T(\tau)\mid\tau)=T(\tau)/T_{\mathrm{max}}$ .

This equivalence isolates invalid-switch rejection. The prefix-context filter $\Psi$ and post-continuation filter $\Phi$ in the recipe intentionally reweight the accepted distribution. For example, let $p_{\Phi}(\tau,t)$ be the probability that a teacher continuation from prefix context $H_{t-1}(\tau)$ passes the post-continuation filter. After applying $\Psi$ and $\Phi$ , the accepted prefix-context distribution becomes

[TABLE A2.E25]
 | $\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mu}^{\rho}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T(\tau)}\Psi(\tau,t)\,p_{\Phi}(\tau,t)\,\mathbf{1}\{H_{t-1}(\tau)\in B\}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mu}^{\rho}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T(\tau)}\Psi(\tau,t)\,p_{\Phi}(\tau,t)\right]}.$ |  | (25)

Invalid-switch rejection alone gives $d^{\rho;\mathrm{pool}}_{\mu}$ ; additional filters produce the corresponding filtered pooled-turn distribution.

As an aside, the above proposal-budget convention is different from topping up every task to the same number of accepted switches. If one resamples until each task has $n$ accepted prefix contexts, invalid-switch rejection instead induces the equal-task average of per-task pooled distributions,

[TABLE A2.E26]
 | $\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{train}}|}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{train}}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{q}^{\rho}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T(\tau)}\mathbf{1}\{H_{t-1}(\tau)\in B\}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{q}^{\rho}}[T(\tau)]},$ |  | (26)

which coincides with the global pooled-turn law only when expected horizons are constant across tasks or when the task distribution is reweighted accordingly. More generally, if $U\sim\nu$ for a fixed distribution on $\{1,\ldots,T_{\mathrm{max}}\}$ , the accepted prefix-context distribution is the corresponding $\nu$ -weighted turn distribution; the uniform case above is exactly pooled-turn sampling.

Appendix G Anatomy of an LLM agent’s rollout

This appendix illustrates how a ReAct-style rollout decomposes into
actions, observations, and tokens, instantiating the formalism in
§ 2.1 .
Figure 7 shows a single rollout on one of our
HotpotQA training questions: a system prompt and question followed by
alternating agent turns (thinking and tool-call segments) and
environment observations (tool responses), with token IDs annotated
so the next-token-prediction target is explicit.
Figure 8 re-draws the same trajectory as a
linearized sequential decision process: the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ emits
each action $a_{t}$ autoregressively conditioned on the growing history $h_{t-1}=(q,a_{1},o_{1},\ldots,a_{t-1},o_{t-1})$ , and the dashed
box marks the next-step prediction target supervised at training
time.
