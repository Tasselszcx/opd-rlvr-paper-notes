Abstract
On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student on its own generated responses using token-level teacher supervision. However, uniform weighting overlooks differences in token learning value, while existing weighting methods rely on predefined mappings from prediction signals to token weights. These mappings are not learned from the effectiveness of the resulting student updates, limiting their ability to adapt to evolving learning needs. In this paper, we propose MetaOPD, a bilevel optimization framework that jointly learns the student model and a lightweight token-weighting network. The inner objective updates the student through weighted OPD, while the outer objective optimizes the weighting network using validation loss on reference solutions after a virtual student update. Differentiating through this update connects weighting decisions to their effects on post-update performance, allowing the mapping from prediction signals to token weights to evolve alongside the student. Experiments on six mathematical reasoning and three out-of-domain datasets, covering two student scales and seven baselines, demonstrate the effectiveness of MetaOPD, with Avg@8/Pass@8 gains over OPD of 1.99/5.97 percentage points for the 0.6B student and 2.25/6.41 points for the 1.7B student.

1 Introduction
Off-policy distillation trains a student on teacher-generated sequences, which can lead to a distribution mismatch between training and inference (Lin et al., 2020; Agarwal et al., 2024). To mitigate this issue, on-policy distillation (OPD) trains the student on its own generated trajectories, with a frozen teacher providing dense token-level supervision. OPD typically employs a mode-seeking reverse-KL objective that encourages the student to capture the teacher’s high-probability behaviors (Wen et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2024). However, experiments by (Li et al., 2026b) show that tokens differ substantially in their learning value, a distinction that standard OPD’s uniform weighting fails to capture.
Recent approaches address this limitation by allocating token-level weights through static proxy rules. For example, EOPD (Jin et al., 2026) uses predictive entropy, while TIP (Xu et al., 2026b) leverages teacher–student disagreement. These methods provide useful signals for allocating supervision based on predictive uncertainty or differences between model predictions. However, their mappings from proxy signals to token weights are predefined rather than adapted to the student’s actual learning gains. As training progresses, the student’s capabilities and generated contexts evolve, and the learning benefit associated with the same proxy signal may change accordingly. Thus, even when token weights vary across inputs, a fixed weighting rule may not transfer effectively across training stages or task contexts. The central challenge is to learn a weighting rule that adapts supervision to the student’s evolving learning needs through feedback from the resulting updates.
To examine whether different token weighting strategies translate into actual learning gains, we conduct a motivating experiment. As illustrated in Figure 1(a), we compare assigning weights through predefined proxies with learning weights. Figure 1(b) reports validation loss reductions relative to Uniform OPD, with larger values indicating greater gains. EOPD yields only a modest improvement, while TIP and random weighting perform close to the uniform baseline. In contrast, learning weights through validation-loss feedback produces a substantially larger improvement. These results suggest that, in this setting, predefined rules do not fully exploit the potential benefits of token weighting, whereas the student’s update performance provides valuable feedback for weight allocation. The key is therefore not only to distinguish tokens using prediction signals, but also to adapt the signal-to-weight mapping based on the effectiveness of the resulting updates. This leads to our central question: How can we use the student’s post-update performance to learn a token weighting rule that translates prediction signals into effective supervision?

In this paper, we take a step forward by proposing MetaOPD, an on-policy distillation framework that jointly optimizes the student model and the token-weighting rule. Specifically, we introduce a lightweight token-weighting network that maps prediction signals to token weights. The student and the weighting network perform two coupled learning tasks: the student improves through weighted teacher supervision, while the weighting network learns to allocate supervision based on the effectiveness of student updates. To achieve this, we formulate their joint learning as a bilevel optimization problem. At the inner level, we update the student on its own generated responses using an OPD loss weighted by the network’s outputs. At the outer level, we evaluate the student’s loss on validation reference solutions after a virtual update and use this loss to optimize the weighting network. Differentiating through the virtual update allows the student’s post-update performance to guide token weight allocation, enabling the network to learn which allocations lead to greater subsequent improvements. Alternating between the two levels allows the student and the weighting rule to evolve together, replacing predefined proxy weighting with adaptive supervision allocation driven by update outcomes. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
•
We identify a gap between static proxy weighting and actual learning gains in OPD, and empirically motivate learning token weights from post-update performance feedback.
•
We propose MetaOPD, a bilevel framework that jointly optimizes the student through weighted OPD and a lightweight token-weighting network through update validation loss.
•
We conduct extensive experiments across six reasoning and three out-of-domain datasets on two student scales against seven baselines, demonstrating the effectiveness of our MetaOPD.
2 Related Work
On-policy distillation (OPD).
On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student to match teacher predictions on student-generated trajectories (Agarwal et al., 2024).
Compared with distillation on fixed data or teacher-generated sequences (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim and Rush, 2016), it reduces training–inference mismatch by providing teacher feedback at prefixes the student actually visits.
One line of work improves OPD through the design of distillation objectives and optimization strategies (Gu et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026).
Another focuses on selecting training prompts, improving rollout trajectories, and refining teacher supervision (Li et al., 2026b; Xu et al., 2026a; Qu et al., 2026).
Since tokens differ in learning value, assigning different weights to tokens within the OPD loss offers another way to improve OPD.
Static proxy weighting OPD.
Static proxy weighting OPD methods start from the observation that dense teacher feedback is not equally useful at every token.
Existing approaches use signals such as entropy and teacher–student disagreement to identify informative tokens and emphasize their supervision through token selection, loss adjustment, or reweighting (Jin et al., 2026; Ko et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026c).
Another line accounts for changes in supervision reliability along a trajectory, using accumulated prefix discrepancy or token position to guide weighting (Xie et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026).
However, these methods use predefined mappings from proxy signals to supervision allocation, without learning from post-update feedback how those mappings should adapt to the student’s evolving learning needs.
Bilevel Optimization.
Bilevel optimization provides a framework for learning example or token weights using feedback on the updated model.
Early meta-reweighting methods used this structure to learn example importance under class imbalance and label noise (Ren et al., 2018; Shu et al., 2019).
Recent studies have extended this principle to language-model training, using outer-loop feedback to replace fixed weighting rules with update-aware supervision (Hu et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026a).
Across these settings, a common view emerges: the value of a training signal is not fixed, but depends on the model update it induces and the reference objective used to assess that update.
MetaOPD applies this principle to token weighting within the OPD loss on student-generated trajectories.
3 Methodology
This section first introduces the token-weighted on-policy distillation objective (§3.1), then presents the bilevel optimization procedure of MetaOPD (§3.2), and finally explains the role of individual tokens in the weighting-network update and describes the gradient computation (§3.3).
3.1 Token-Weighted On-Policy Distillation
Let $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}$ denote the training distribution over question–solution pairs.
We write $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}^{X}$ for the corresponding distribution of questions. The student $\pi_{\theta}$ has trainable parameters $\theta$ , and $\pi_{\mathrm{T}}$ is the frozen teacher. At the start of each rollout round, we set $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}\leftarrow\theta$ . For $x_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}^{X}$ , we sample a response from $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ :
|
| $y_{i}=(a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,T_{i}})\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot\mid x_{i}),\qquad s_{i,t}=(x_{i},a_{i,<t}),$
|
| (1)
where $s_{i,t}$ is the visited prefix and $a_{i,t}$ the sampled token. The mask $m_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}$ marks valid completion positions, including an observed end-of-sequence token.
Figure 2: Overview of MetaOPD.
(1) Standard OPD uses uniform token weights, while (2) static proxy weighting OPD assigns token weights through predefined rules based on proxy signals.
(3) MetaOPD uses two branches: (a) the weighting network learns from the student’s validation loss after a virtual weighted OPD update; (b) the updated network recomputes weights for the actual student update. The schematic sums omit valid-token normalization, and weights in the actual-update branch refer to those recomputed after the weighting-network update.
For each sampled token, write $u^{\theta}_{i,t}=\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{i,t}\mid s_{i,t})$ , with $u^{\mathrm{old}}_{i,t}$ denoting the rollout-time log-probability and $u^{\mathrm{T}}_{i,t}$ the teacher log-probability after aligning token identities. All three use full-vocabulary normalization; Appendix A.1 specifies the teacher-view mapping. The detached teacher-minus-rollout log-probability difference and the current-to-old probability ratio are
|
| $A_{i,t}=\operatorname{sg}[u^{\mathrm{T}}_{i,t}-u^{\mathrm{old}}_{i,t}],\qquad r_{i,t}(\theta)=\exp(u^{\theta}_{i,t}-u^{\mathrm{old}}_{i,t}),$
|
| (2)
where $\operatorname{sg}$ preserves its argument’s value but blocks gradients through it. Following the sampled reverse-KL update used in Relay-OPD (Xu et al., 2026a), let $r^{\mathrm{clip}}_{i,t}=\operatorname{clip}(r_{i,t},1-\epsilon_{l},1+\epsilon_{h})$ , with clipping margins $\epsilon_{l},\epsilon_{h}$ , and define the per-token OPD term as
|
| $\ell^{\mathrm{OPD}}_{i,t}(\theta)=\max\!\left(-A_{i,t}r_{i,t}(\theta),-A_{i,t}r^{\mathrm{clip}}_{i,t}\right).$
|
| (3)
At $\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$ , the unclipped form gives an unbiased single-action estimate of the student-to-teacher reverse-KL gradient at the fixed visited prefix, using the induced teacher distribution defined in Appendix A.1. Away from that policy, the cached log-probability difference and clipping define a local surrogate.
A shared weighting network $q_{\eta}$ , with trainable parameters $\eta$ , maps each token’s descriptor $f_{i,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{74}$ to a scalar score. The descriptor contains student and teacher log-probabilities of the sampled token, entropy, prediction agreement, position, and ranked top-32 log-probability profiles (Table 4). These profiles are inputs only and do not restrict the OPD loss vocabulary. The feature $\delta_{i,t}=u^{\mathrm{T}}_{i,t}-u^{\theta}_{i,t}$ uses current-student log-probabilities, whereas $A_{i,t}$ uses cached rollout-policy log-probabilities. All numeric inputs are detached; descriptors are constructed without reference solutions.
We convert the network scores into positive weights with unit mean within each response. For $n_{i}=\sum_{t}m_{i,t}>0$ , define
|
| $\displaystyle z_{i,t}$
| $\displaystyle=q_{\eta}(\operatorname{sg}[f_{i,t}]),$
| $\displaystyle\bar{z}_{i}$
| $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{s}m_{i,s}z_{i,s},$
|
| (4)
|
| $\displaystyle\widetilde{w}_{\eta,i,t}$
| $\displaystyle=1+\rho\tanh(z_{i,t}-\bar{z}_{i}),$
| $\displaystyle w_{\eta,i,t}$
| $\displaystyle=m_{i,t}\widetilde{w}_{\eta,i,t}\frac{n_{i}}{\sum_{s}m_{i,s}\widetilde{w}_{\eta,i,s}}.$
|
Centering expresses each token’s score relative to the mean over valid tokens in the same response. Normalization then keeps the mean weight over these tokens at one, allowing the network to adjust their relative contributions within the OPD loss. Both operations are differentiated with respect to $\eta$ . We use residual scale $\rho=0.25$ and a zero-initialized output head, so all valid weights start at one. Completely masked responses receive zero weights. The architecture, weight bounds, and numerical safeguards appear in Appendices A.2 and B.
For an optimizer minibatch $B$ , the weighted OPD objective is
|
| $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}}(\theta,\eta;B)=\frac{1}{N_{B}}\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}w_{\eta,i,t}\ell^{\mathrm{OPD}}_{i,t}(\theta),\qquad N_{B}=\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}.$
|
| (5)
The numerator and valid-token count are reduced over the complete optimizer minibatch. Setting all valid weights to one recovers uniform sampled OPD.
3.2 Bilevel Optimization of MetaOPD
Figure 2 illustrates the bilevel procedure: the student learns from weighted OPD, while the weighting network learns from the student’s validation loss after a virtual student update. Here validation loss is the teacher-forced token loss on training reference solutions, used as the outer objective defined below. Following gradient-based meta-reweighting and Meta-Weight-Net (Ren et al., 2018; Shu et al., 2019), each optimizer minibatch involves a virtual student update, a weighting-network update, and an actual student update.
Virtual student update.
Teacher statistics are cached with the rollout. At the start of each optimizer minibatch, we compute current-student statistics and detach the descriptors used to obtain the token weights. Let $\mathsf{S}$ collect the incoming student AdamW moments, step counters, and parameter-group hyperparameters. Write $\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{in}}(\eta):=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}}(\theta,\eta;B)$ for the student gradient, whose dependence on $\eta$ is through the token weights. One virtual step defines the virtual student parameters
|
| $\theta^{\prime}(\eta)=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{AdamW}}\!\left(\theta,\mathcal{C}\!\left(\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{in}}(\eta)\right);\mathsf{S}\right).$
|
| (6)
The virtual and actual student steps use the same AdamW update rule (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017). Here $\mathcal{C}$ applies full-model global-norm clipping, and $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{AdamW}}$ reads the current student optimizer state and scheduled learning rates. Differentiation with respect to $\eta$ holds $\theta$ , $\mathsf{S}$ , the rollout, and detached descriptors fixed. The dependence through the token weights and student gradient is retained, including the new virtual moments, clipping coefficient, and AdamW parameter map. This operation changes neither the real parameters nor the stored moments, step counters, or scheduler. Appendix A.3 expands the two maps.
Weighting-network update.
Draw a batch $B^{\mathrm{ref}}=\{(x_{j}^{\star},y_{j}^{\star})\}$ of prompts and reference solutions from a separately shuffled stream over $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}$ ; overlap with the rollout stream is allowed. At $\theta^{\prime}(\eta)$ , the student predicts each solution token given the prompt and preceding solution tokens. We compute validation loss under teacher forcing, averaging over valid target tokens:
|
| $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}(\theta^{\prime};B^{\mathrm{ref}})=-\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{ref}}}\sum_{j,t}m^{\mathrm{ref}}_{j,t}\log\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}(y_{j,t}^{\star}\mid x_{j}^{\star},y_{j,<t}^{\star}),\qquad N_{\mathrm{ref}}=\sum_{j,t}m^{\mathrm{ref}}_{j,t}.$
|
| (7)
The mask $m^{\mathrm{ref}}_{j,t}$ selects valid reference-solution tokens; $N_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is counted separately from $N_{B}$ .
To regularize departures from uniform weighting, define
|
| $\mathcal{R}_{w}(\eta;B)=\frac{1}{N_{B}}\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}(w_{\eta,i,t}-1)^{2}.$
|
| (8)
At global student step $r$ , let $\lambda_{w}^{(r)}$ be the weight-regularization coefficient, fixed for this step. We optimize the one-step meta-objective
|
| $\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{meta}}(\eta)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}(\theta^{\prime}(\eta);B^{\mathrm{ref}})+\lambda_{w}^{(r)}\mathcal{R}_{w}(\eta;B).$
|
| (9)
Applying the chain rule to the meta-objective gives the meta-gradient with respect to $\eta$ :
|
| $\displaystyle\nabla_{\eta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{meta}}$
| $\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial\theta^{\prime}(\eta)}{\partial\eta}\right)^{\top}\nabla_{\theta^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}\bigl(\theta^{\prime}(\eta);B^{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr)+\lambda_{w}^{(r)}\nabla_{\eta}\mathcal{R}_{w}(\eta;B).$
|
| (10)
The first term propagates the validation-loss gradient through the virtual update; the second comes from the weight regularizer. Let $\mathsf{M}$ denote the weighting network’s AdamW state and $\operatorname{AdamWStep}$ an update returning parameters and state. One update per student minibatch gives
|
| $(\eta^{+},\mathsf{M}^{+})=\operatorname{AdamWStep}_{\eta}(\eta,\nabla_{\eta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{meta}};\mathsf{M}).$
|
| (11)
Section 3.3 expands this gradient into token contributions and describes its computation. The weighting-network learning rate and $\lambda_{w}^{(r)}$ follow the student learning-rate schedule in Appendix B.4.
Actual student update.
We discard the virtual parameters and recompute token weights with $q_{\eta^{+}}$ on the same detached descriptors. The actual student gradient uses these weights as constants:
|
| $\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{real}}$
| $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{B}}\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}\operatorname{sg}[w_{\eta^{+},i,t}]\nabla_{\theta}\ell^{\mathrm{OPD}}_{i,t}(\theta),$
|
| (12)
|
| $\displaystyle(\theta^{+},\mathsf{S}^{+})$
| $\displaystyle=\operatorname{AdamWStep}_{\theta}(\theta,\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{real}});\mathsf{S}).$
|
This step starts from the original $(\theta,\mathsf{S})$ , not from $\theta^{\prime}$ , and advances the real optimizer state.
Its objective contains only weighted OPD supervision on student-generated responses; validation loss trains the weighting network through the meta-objective.

3.3 Token-Level Interpretation and Gradient Computation
We examine how a small change in a token’s weight within the OPD loss affects validation loss after the virtual student update. Throughout, $\nabla_{\eta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}$ denotes the gradient with respect to $\eta$ obtained by differentiating $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}(\theta^{\prime}(\eta);B^{\mathrm{ref}})$ through the virtual update, with the inputs fixed as in §3.2.
Define the token gradient $\boldsymbol{g}_{i,t}=\nabla_{\theta}\ell^{\mathrm{OPD}}_{i,t}(\theta)$ . The detached descriptors make weights constant with respect to $\theta$ , while preserving their dependence on $\eta$ , so
|
| $\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{in}}(\eta)=\frac{1}{N_{B}}\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}w_{\eta,i,t}\boldsymbol{g}_{i,t}.$
|
| (13)
Let $\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{out}}=\nabla_{\theta^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}(\theta^{\prime};B^{\mathrm{ref}})$ , evaluated at $\theta^{\prime}=\theta^{\prime}(\eta)$ .
Write $J_{\mathcal{C}}$ for the clipping Jacobian at $\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{in}}(\eta)$ , and $J_{\mathcal{A}}$ for the AdamW parameter-map Jacobian with respect to its gradient input, evaluated at $\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{in}}(\eta))$ . Propagating the outer gradient through these maps defines
|
| $\boldsymbol{d}=-J_{\mathcal{C}}^{\top}J_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{out}}.$
|
| (14)
This derivative-based vector, rather than the actual student parameter change, determines each token’s contribution to the weighting-network gradient:
|
| $c_{i,t}=\langle\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{g}_{i,t}\rangle,\qquad\nabla_{\eta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}=-\frac{1}{N_{B}}\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}c_{i,t}\nabla_{\eta}w_{\eta,i,t}.$
|
| (15)
For a valid token, $\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}/\partial w_{i,t}=-c_{i,t}/N_{B}$ when the other normalized-weight coordinates are held fixed. Thus, $c_{i,t}>0$ means that a small increase in this weight alone locally reduces validation loss.
Learning rates are already included in $J_{\mathcal{A}}$ ; adding $\lambda_{w}^{(r)}\nabla_{\eta}\mathcal{R}_{w}$ to Equation 15 gives the complete meta-gradient in Equation 10. Appendix A.4 provides the intermediate Jacobian and chain-rule steps.
Response normalization couples the weight coordinates. Suppressing $\eta$ for clarity, let $\overline{w}_{i}=n_{i}^{-1}\sum_{t}m_{i,t}\widetilde{w}_{i,t}$ . Differentiating $w_{i,t}=\widetilde{w}_{i,t}/\overline{w}_{i}$ on valid positions gives
|
| $\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}}{\partial\widetilde{w}_{i,j}}=-\frac{c_{i,j}-\bar{c}_{i}^{\,w}}{N_{B}\overline{w}_{i}},\qquad\bar{c}_{i}^{\,w}=\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{t}m_{i,t}w_{i,t}c_{i,t}.$
|
| (16)
The gradient with respect to a raw weight therefore depends on $c_{i,j}-\bar{c}_{i}^{\,w}$ , the difference from the response’s weighted mean. Score centering, the shared network, and regularization further affect the update, so $c_{i,j}>0$ need not lead to an increase in the learned weight. This derivative describes a local effect; it does not guarantee finite-step or long-term improvement. Appendix A.2 expands the normalization derivative.
Reverse-mode vector–Jacobian products (VJPs) first compute $\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{out}}$ , then propagate it through AdamW and clipping to obtain $\boldsymbol{d}$ . A Jacobian–vector product (JVP) of the vector of per-token OPD terms at the current $\theta$ , in direction $\boldsymbol{d}$ , gives all $c_{i,t}$ values without materializing the token-by-parameter Jacobian. Recomputing weights with their $\eta$ -dependence intact, we differentiate the following objective while treating $c_{i,t}$ as constants:
|
| $\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{meta}}^{(r)}(\eta;B,\boldsymbol{c})=-\frac{1}{N_{B}}\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}w_{\eta,i,t}\operatorname{sg}[c_{i,t}]+\lambda_{w}^{(r)}\mathcal{R}_{w}(\eta;B).$
|
| (17)
Holding $c_{i,t}$ fixed retains the Jacobian-product values required by the chain rule without differentiating them again. The resulting $\eta$ -gradient equals the complete gradient in Equation 10 at the iterate where these values were computed; this is a first-derivative equality at that point, not a global identity between objectives. Appendix A.4 verifies the equality.

4 Experiments
4.1 Experimental Setup
Datasets. We evaluate on six mathematical reasoning benchmarks, i.e., MATH500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Lightman et al., 2024), AMC23 (Yang et al., 2024), Minerva Math (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), HMMT 2025 (Balunović et al., 2025), AIME24 (Zhang and Math-AI, 2024), and AIME25 (Zhang and Math-AI, 2025), and assess out-of-domain on three additional datasets, i.e., GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al., 2023), MMLU Pro (Wang et al., 2024), and AlpacaEval 2.0 (Dubois et al., 2024).
Baseline methods. We compare our MetaOPD with seven baselines spanning different learning strategies: KD (Hinton et al., 2015), GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), OPD (Agarwal et al., 2024), EOPD (Jin et al., 2026),
FiRe-OPD (Li et al., 2026c),
REOPOLD (Ko et al., 2026), and TIP (Xu et al., 2026b).
Implementation details. We use Qwen3 (Yang et al., 2025) models, with Qwen3-8B as the frozen teacher and Qwen3-0.6B-Base and Qwen3-1.7B-Base as students. For all methods, both student models are trained for three epochs on MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021).
MetaOPD samples rollout prompts and reference solutions from independently shuffled versions of the training split, allowing overlap between the two streams.
Evaluation examples are excluded from both student and weighting network updates. All methods are evaluated using the same decoding settings and a Math-Verify-based answer verifier. We generate eight responses per problem and report Avg@8, the average accuracy across these responses, and Pass@8, the proportion of problems with at least one correct response. We aggregate each metric using an unweighted average across benchmarks. Full training and evaluation details are provided in Appendix B.

Mathematical-reasoning performance.

MetaOPD achieves the highest six-benchmark averages for both Avg@8 and Pass@8 at both student scales, including comparisons with adaptive OPD baselines. With the 1.7B student, MetaOPD improves Avg@8/Pass@8 over standard OPD by 2.25/6.41 percentage points and over TIP, which attains the highest aggregate scores among the adaptive OPD baselines, by 1.13/1.56 points. Out-of-Domain Generalization.
We further evaluate the MATH-trained Qwen3-1.7B-Base on GPQA-Diamond, MMLU Pro, and AlpacaEval 2.0, without additional training. 4.3 Ablations and Controls
How reference solutions are used.
We further examine whether the gains of MetaOPD can be attributed to direct supervision from the reference solutions used in the meta-objective. We compare against SFT on reference solutions and variants that add the SFT loss directly to the student objective. The reference coefficient is determined by matching the initial gradient norms of the OPD and SFT losses using training data only, without benchmark-based tuning (Appendix B.6). SFT alone underperforms both OPD and MetaOPD in aggregate performance (Table 9). Moreover, adding the SFT loss to uniform OPD increases Pass@8 but consistently reduces Avg@8 across all three tested coefficients (Figure 3(a)), whereas MetaOPD improves both metrics. Adding the same SFT loss to MetaOPD further decreases Avg@8 and Pass@8 by 5.87 and 6.72 points, respectively (Figure 3(b)). These controls show that the tested forms of direct supervision do not reproduce MetaOPD’s gains in both metrics, supporting the use of validation loss after the student update to optimize the token-weighting rule.

5 Conclusion
On-policy distillation provides dense teacher feedback on student rollouts, but local uncertainty or disagreement alone does not determine whether assigning a token a higher weight within the OPD loss will improve student learning.
We presented MetaOPD, a one-step bilevel framework that learns to allocate token-level teacher supervision using the student’s validation loss after a virtual distillation update. By differentiating this loss on reference solutions through the virtual update, MetaOPD trains a shared weighting network whose token weights guide the student’s actual OPD update on its own rollouts.
Across six mathematical-reasoning benchmarks and two Qwen3 student scales, MetaOPD achieves the highest six-benchmark averages for Avg@8 and Pass@8 among the compared methods. Ablations and one-step update comparisons support learning token weights within the OPD loss using feedback on the updated student. Extending these findings to broader models and tasks, relating local update quality to long-horizon learning, and reducing meta-update cost remain open directions.

$u^{\mathrm{T}}(a)=\log\pi_{\mathrm{T}}(\mu(a)\mid\mathcal{T}(s))$ . Both $\mathcal{T}(s)$ and
$Z_{\mu}(s)$ are fixed with respect to the student parameters in this differentiation. Then
$\log\pi_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mu}(a\mid s)=u^{\mathrm{T}}(a)-\log Z_{\mu}(s)$ . The corresponding
full-vocabulary reverse KL is
|
| $\KL\!\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s)\,\middle\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mu}(\cdot\mid s)\right)=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[u^{\theta}(a)-u^{\mathrm{T}}(a)+\log Z_{\mu}(s)\right].$
|
Differentiating both the sampling distribution and its log-density gives
|
| $\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\KL(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mu})$
| $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\left(u^{\theta}(a)-u^{\mathrm{T}}(a)+\log Z_{\mu}(s)+1\right)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s)\right]$
|
|
|
| $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\left(u^{\theta}(a)-u^{\mathrm{T}}(a)\right)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s)\right],$
|
| (19)
because both $1$ and $\log Z_{\mu}(s)$ are action-independent at the fixed prefix and the expected
score $\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s)]$ is zero.
At $\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$ , $r=1$ and
|
| $\left.\nabla_{\theta}[-A(a)r(a;\theta)]\right|_{\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}=\left(u^{\mathrm{old}}(a)-u^{\mathrm{T}}(a)\right)\left.\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s)\right|_{\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{old}}},$
|
so a behavior-policy action provides an unbiased single-sample estimator of
Equation 19. This statement is conditional on the visited prefix and does not
differentiate the trajectory distribution.
Away from the rollout policy, the objective with the cached $A$ held fixed has expectation
|
| $\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\!\left[-A(a)r(a;\theta)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\theta}}\!\left[u^{\mathrm{old}}(a)-u^{\mathrm{T}}(a)\right].$
|
It therefore differs from the current reverse KL, whose score coefficient contains
$u^{\theta}(a)-u^{\mathrm{T}}(a)$ . The importance ratio supports a local update from cached
behavior-policy actions; it does not restore exact reverse-KL equivalence at an arbitrary
$\theta$ .
The active branch of the clipped loss depends on the sign of $A$ . For $A>0$ , it is
$-A\min(r,1+\epsilon_{h})$ , so an excessive probability increase is clipped. For $A<0$ , it is
$-A\max(r,1-\epsilon_{l})$ , so an excessive probability decrease is clipped. At a clipping
boundary, automatic differentiation supplies the implementation-defined subgradient.
A.2 Score Centering and Response Normalization
For response $i$ , let $n_{i}=\sum_{t}m_{i,t}$ . Under the convention following Equation 4, all normalized weights are zero when $n_{i}=0$ . Below, assume $n_{i}>0$ and define
|
| $\overline{w}_{i}=\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{t}m_{i,t}\widetilde{w}_{i,t}.$
|
Then Equation 4 is $w_{i,t}=\widetilde{w}_{i,t}/\overline{w}_{i}$ on valid
positions, which immediately gives
|
| $\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{t}m_{i,t}w_{i,t}=1.$
|
With $\widetilde{w}\in(1-\rho,1+\rho)$ , the normalized weights satisfy
|
| $w_{i,t}\in\left(\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho},\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}\right).$
|
With $\rho=0.25$ , the normalized range is $w_{i,t}\in(0.6,5/3)$ , even though the final
weights need not remain inside the raw interval $(0.75,1.25)$ .
For valid positions $t$ and $j$ ,
|
| $\frac{\partial w_{i,t}}{\partial\widetilde{w}_{i,j}}=\frac{\mathbb{I}[t=j]}{\overline{w}_{i}}-\frac{\widetilde{w}_{i,t}}{n_{i}\overline{w}_{i}^{2}},$
|
which makes the within-response coupling explicit. Combining this derivative with
Equation 15 gives the gradient with respect to the unnormalized weights in
Equation 16, expanded here for a valid raw weight:
|
| $\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}}{\partial\widetilde{w}_{i,j}}=-\frac{1}{N_{B}\overline{w}_{i}}\left(c_{i,j}-\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{t}m_{i,t}w_{i,t}c_{i,t}\right).$
|
Here $c_{i,t}$ is defined in Equation 15; its
learning-rate dependence is already included in $\boldsymbol{d}$ . Thus normalization subtracts
the response-level weighted mean from $c_{i,j}$ , rather than using its sign alone.
Invalid weights are zero, so a completely masked response contributes to
neither the weighted loss nor the weight regularizer. The implementation also clamps valid counts
below by one and rejects optimizer minibatches with $N_{B}=0$ .
Score centering introduces a second coupling before the residual map. On the valid positions
of response $i$ , let $\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}$ collect the network scores and define
|
| $P_{i}=I-\frac{1}{n_{i}}\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{\top},\qquad\boldsymbol{z}_{i}^{\circ}=P_{i}\boldsymbol{z}_{i},\qquad D_{i}=\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\rho[1-\tanh^{2}(z_{i,t}^{\circ})]\right).$
|
Writing $J_{i}$ for the normalization Jacobian above, the complete derivative is
|
| $\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{w}_{i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{z}_{i}}=J_{i}D_{i}P_{i},\qquad\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}=-\frac{1}{N_{B}}P_{i}D_{i}J_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{c}_{i}.$
|
| (20)
Both $P_{i}$ and $D_{i}$ are symmetric. In particular, the score derivative includes the
mean-score derivative; detaching that mean would change the meta-gradient. A common score
shift is annihilated by $P_{i}$ , and a response with one valid token has constant weight one.
The same Jacobian propagates the regularizer derivative, with
$\nabla_{\boldsymbol{w}_{i}}\mathcal{R}_{w}=2(\boldsymbol{w}_{i}-\boldsymbol{1})/N_{B}$ .
The implementation safeguards the normalization denominator by $10^{-8}$ ; since
$\sum_{t}m_{i,t}\widetilde{w}_{i,t}>n_{i}(1-\rho)$ , this safeguard is inactive for nonempty responses.
A.3 AdamW Virtual Update
Consider one optimizer minibatch and suppress its training index. For each trainable parameter
tensor $\theta_{j}$ , the current real optimizer supplies first and second moments
$\boldsymbol{m}_{j},\boldsymbol{v}_{j}$ , a completed-step count $\tau_{j}$ , and its parameter-group
settings $(\alpha_{j},\beta_{1,j},\beta_{2,j},\varepsilon_{j},\lambda_{j})$ : learning rate, moment
decays, denominator epsilon, and decoupled weight decay. These quantities form $\mathsf{S}$ in
Equation 6. The virtual step reads them as constants with respect to $\eta$ .
Zero initialization is used only if the corresponding real optimizer state is uninitialized;
moments and step counts are not reset at subsequent virtual steps. We assume the standard
settings $0\leq\beta_{1,j}<1$ , $0<\beta_{2,j}<1$ , and $\varepsilon_{j}>0$ .
Global gradient clipping.
The input to clipping is the full-model gradient of the global token-mean inner loss,
after microbatch accumulation and distributed reduction. Let $G_{\max}>0$ be the real update’s
norm threshold and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\geq 0$ its numerical safeguard. For
$n=\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{in}}(\eta)\|_{2}>0$ , define
|
| $\kappa_{\mathrm{clip}}=\min\!\left(1,\frac{G_{\max}}{n+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}}\right),\qquad\overline{\boldsymbol{g}}_{\mathrm{in}}(\eta)=\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{in}}(\eta))=\kappa_{\mathrm{clip}}\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{in}}(\eta).$
|
| (21)
The zero gradient maps to zero. Clipping uses one norm over all trainable student parameters,
including parameters in different optimizer groups; it is not applied separately to each
microbatch or parameter tensor. On a smooth branch, its Jacobian is
|
| $J_{\mathcal{C}}=\begin{cases}I,&n+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}<G_{\max},\\[2.0pt]
\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathrm{clip}}I-\frac{G_{\max}}{n(n+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}})^{2}}\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{in}}(\eta)\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{in}}(\eta)^{\top},&n+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}>G_{\max},\ n>0.\end{cases}$
|
| (22)
At the switching boundary, the derivative follows the branch convention of the real clipping
operation. The rank-one term accounts for the dependence of the clipping coefficient on the
weights through $\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{in}}(\eta)$ . Detaching that coefficient would omit this term.
At the origin, $J_{\mathcal{C}}=\min(1,G_{\max}/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}})I$ for
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}>0$ , and $J_{\mathcal{C}}=I$ when the safeguard is zero.
If clipping is disabled, $\mathcal{C}$ and $J_{\mathcal{C}}$ are the identity.
Moment update and parameter map.
For standard AdamW, the virtual moments, bias corrections, and virtual student parameters are
|
| $\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}_{j}^{+}$
| $\displaystyle=\beta_{1,j}\boldsymbol{m}_{j}+(1-\beta_{1,j})\overline{\boldsymbol{g}}_{\mathrm{in},j}(\eta),$
|
| (23)
|
| $\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{+}$
| $\displaystyle=\beta_{2,j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}+(1-\beta_{2,j})\overline{\boldsymbol{g}}_{\mathrm{in},j}(\eta)^{\,2},$
|
|
| $\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}_{j}$
| $\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{m}_{j}^{+}}{1-\beta_{1,j}^{\,\tau_{j}+1}},$
| $\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}$
| $\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{+}}{1-\beta_{2,j}^{\,\tau_{j}+1}},$
|
|
| $\displaystyle\theta^{\prime}_{j}$
| $\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda_{j})\theta_{j}-\alpha_{j}\frac{\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}_{j}}{\sqrt{\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}}+\varepsilon_{j}}.$
|
All products, squares, square roots, and divisions within a parameter tensor are elementwise.
The learning rate $\alpha_{j}$ is read from the current real parameter group, including scheduled
decay. Bias correction uses that tensor’s next step $\tau_{j}+1$ . The expressions above create
temporary values; they do not overwrite $\theta_{j}$ , $\boldsymbol{m}_{j}$ , $\boldsymbol{v}_{j}$ ,
or $\tau_{j}$ . Only the later real student step advances the stored moments and step counts.
For clarity, the AdamW Jacobian can also be written coordinatewise. Let
|
| $\zeta_{1,j}=\frac{1-\beta_{1,j}}{1-\beta_{1,j}^{\,\tau_{j}+1}},\qquad\zeta_{2,j}=\frac{1-\beta_{2,j}}{1-\beta_{2,j}^{\,\tau_{j}+1}},\qquad\sigma_{j,k}=\sqrt{\widehat{v}_{j,k}},$
|
where $k$ indexes a coordinate of tensor $j$ . For $\sigma_{j,k}>0$ ,
|
| $\frac{\partial\theta^{\prime}_{j,k}}{\partial\overline{g}_{\mathrm{in},j,k}(\eta)}=-\alpha_{j}\left[\frac{\zeta_{1,j}}{\sigma_{j,k}+\varepsilon_{j}}-\frac{\zeta_{2,j}\widehat{m}_{j,k}\overline{g}_{\mathrm{in},j,k}(\eta)}{\sigma_{j,k}(\sigma_{j,k}+\varepsilon_{j})^{2}}\right].$
|
| (24)
The off-diagonal entries of $J_{\mathcal{A}}$ are zero. The second term differentiates the
new second moment; treating AdamW as a fixed preconditioner would discard it. At a zero-gradient
coordinate with zero first and second moments, the composite map has derivative
$-\alpha_{j}\zeta_{1,j}/\varepsilon_{j}$ , obtained by continuity rather than division by
$\sigma_{j,k}=0$ . Decoupled weight decay has no direct derivative with respect to the gradient
input because $\theta_{j}$ is fixed. It still changes $\theta^{\prime}$ , and hence the outer gradient,
so it must remain in the forward virtual map.
A.4 One-Step Hypergradient and VJP–JVP Form
We derive the token decomposition in §3.3 from the complete meta-gradient in Equation 10. Vectorize the student and weighting parameters as $\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ and $\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{q}$ . Gradients are column vectors; the Jacobian of a map from $\mathbb{R}^{q}$ to $\mathbb{R}^{p}$ has shape $p\times q$ , with rows indexing outputs and columns indexing inputs. We use the fixed-input convention of §3.2: current student parameters, real optimizer state, rollout actions, masks, old/teacher caches, and detached descriptors are held fixed.
Only the normalized weights in Equation 13 depend on $\eta$ . Since $\boldsymbol{g}_{i,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ and $\nabla_{\eta}w_{\eta,i,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}$ , differentiating each weighted term gives the outer product
|
| $G_{\eta}:=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{in}}(\eta)}{\partial\eta}=\frac{1}{N_{B}}\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}\boldsymbol{g}_{i,t}\left(\nabla_{\eta}w_{\eta,i,t}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q},\qquad\frac{\partial\theta^{\prime}}{\partial\eta}=J_{\mathcal{A}}J_{\mathcal{C}}G_{\eta}.$
|
| (25)
The two optimizer Jacobians are $p\times p$ , and $\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{out}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ . Thus the validation-loss term of Equation 10 is the $q$ -vector
|
| $\nabla_{\eta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}=\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{in}}(\eta)}{\partial\eta}\right)^{\top}J_{\mathcal{C}}^{\top}J_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{out}}=G_{\eta}^{\top}J_{\mathcal{C}}^{\top}J_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{out}}.$
|
| (26)
Using $J_{\mathcal{C}}^{\top}J_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{out}}=-\boldsymbol{d}$ from Equation 14 and expanding $G_{\eta}^{\top}$ yields
|
| $\nabla_{\eta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}=-\frac{1}{N_{B}}\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}\left(\nabla_{\eta}w_{\eta,i,t}\right)\boldsymbol{g}_{i,t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}=-\frac{1}{N_{B}}\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}c_{i,t}\nabla_{\eta}w_{\eta,i,t}.$
|
| (27)
Here $\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ and $c_{i,t}=\boldsymbol{g}_{i,t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}$ is scalar, establishing Equation 15.
To verify the gradient of Equation 17, fix an evaluation point $\eta_{0}$ and write $c^{0}_{i,t}=c_{i,t}(\eta_{0})$ . The VJP–JVP procedure in §3.3 supplies these values. Differentiating the objective while holding them fixed gives
|
| $\displaystyle\left.\nabla_{\eta}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{meta}}^{(r)}(\eta;B,\boldsymbol{c}^{0})\right|_{\eta_{0}}$
| $\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N_{B}}\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}c^{0}_{i,t}\left.\nabla_{\eta}w_{\eta,i,t}\right|_{\eta_{0}}+\lambda_{w}^{(r)}\left.\nabla_{\eta}\mathcal{R}_{w}\right|_{\eta_{0}}$
|
|
|
| $\displaystyle=\left.\nabla_{\eta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{meta}}\right|_{\eta_{0}}.$
|
The second equality uses Equation 27. Exactness concerns the specified single-step map, including its new moments and clipping coefficient, under the fixed-input convention above. At clipping boundaries, the derivative follows the corresponding automatic-differentiation convention.

B.2.1 Token-Allocation Baseline Implementations

The underlying surrogate form, student optimizer, and training schedule are shared; method-specific selection, weighting, reward processing, and loss reduction are specified below. This design focuses the comparison on supervision allocation while controlling for differences in the original methods’ underlying OPD objectives.

|

REOPOLD.
For the detached reward $R_{i,t}=u^{\mathrm{T}}_{i,t}-u^{\mathrm{old}}_{i,t}$ , define $R_{\min}=\log(\lambda)/(1-\lambda)$ . We replace $A_{i,t}$ in Equation 3 with $\max(R_{i,t},R_{\min})$ . At zero-based updates 0–231, tokens with $R_{i,t}\geq R_{\min}$ are retained. At updates 232–695, selection instead retains the $\lceil 0.2N_{B}\rceil$ highest-student-entropy tokens across the optimizer minibatch. If a response would be empty, its highest-reward token in phase I or highest-entropy token in phase II is retained. This safeguard can raise the actual retained fraction above 0.2 in phase II.
TIP Soft-OR.
The two signals are $h_{i,t}=H(\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot\mid s_{i,t}))/\log|\mathcal{V}|$ and the exact full-vocabulary $d_{i,t}=\KL(\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}\|\pi_{\mathrm{T}})$ at the corresponding student and teacher context views. The latter is computed from full logits, rather than from the sampled-token log-probability gap. We cap $h$ at its minibatch 98th percentile, then separately min–max normalize the capped entropy and $d$ to obtain $\hat{h}$ and $\hat{d}$ . A signal whose range is at most FP32 machine epsilon maps to zero. The score is $s_{i,t}=\hat{h}_{i,t}+\hat{d}_{i,t}-\hat{h}_{i,t}\hat{d}_{i,t}$ . Each response retains its $\max(1,\lfloor 0.5n_{i}\rfloor)$ highest-scoring valid tokens. Ranking ties in all three methods preserve the gathered input order. Full-vocabulary reverse KL is used for token selection; the student update on retained tokens uses the shared sampled-OPD surrogate in Equation 3 in place of TIP’s original full-vocabulary reverse-KL training loss.
Reduction after selection.
For all three baselines, the loss is the weighted sum of retained-token surrogate losses divided by the total number of retained tokens in the global optimizer minibatch. FiRe-OPD uses its response-normalized soft weights; REOPOLD and TIP use unit weights on retained tokens and zero weights elsewhere. The binary masks are not rescaled to have mean one over the original response. Thus, filtering changes both the numerator and the token-count denominator.
B.3 Global Token-Mean Reduction
Both the virtual and actual student updates evaluate the weighted OPD loss using the global valid-token count $N_{B}$ from the complete 32-example optimizer minibatch. The numerator is accumulated across all local microbatches and distributed ranks before division by $N_{B}$ ; the implementation does not average independently normalized microbatch losses. Response-level weight normalization in Equation 4 is applied before this global reduction.
To compute validation loss, we similarly sum token losses on reference solutions over the microbatches and participating student ranks, then divide by the global valid reference-solution token count $N_{\mathrm{ref}}$ . Because completion lengths differ, $N_{B}$ and $N_{\mathrm{ref}}$ are generally different even though both global example batches contain 32 examples.
The inner gradient is globally reduced before norm clipping and the virtual optimizer map; the outer gradient is globally reduced before the optimizer VJPs. Clipping each rank’s partial gradient would define a different virtual update.
B.4 Virtual State and Learning-Rate Convention
As specified in §3.2, training-time virtual steps use the current student AdamW state and scheduled learning rates without committing optimizer or parameter changes.
The motivating comparison in Appendix C starts from zero student moments and step zero. The checkpoint probes in Appendix D restore the trained student optimizer state. Both use a fixed reference learning rate of $3\times 10^{-6}$ , whereas training-time virtual steps read the scheduled rate.
Coupled learning-rate schedules.
Let $\alpha_{g}^{(r)}$ be the live student learning rate in trainable parameter group $g$
at global student step $r$ , and let $\alpha_{0}=3\times 10^{-6}$ be the configured initial rate.
The schedule scale and effective meta settings are
|
| $\chi_{r}=\min\!\left(1,\max\!\left(0,\max_{g}\frac{\alpha_{g}^{(r)}}{\alpha_{0}}\right)\right),\quad\alpha_{\eta}^{(r)}=\alpha_{\eta,0}\chi_{r},\quad\lambda_{w}^{(r)}=\lambda_{w}^{(0)}\chi_{r},$
|
| (28)
where $\alpha_{\eta,0}=2.5\times 10^{-5}$ and $\lambda_{w}^{(0)}=10^{-3}$ .
The student uses cosine decay, advanced once per rollout round; all four optimizer minibatches
in that round read its current rate. The weighting network uses AdamW with zero weight decay and is
updated at each student step throughout all 696 updates. The schedule values are constants

B.5 End-of-Sequence Mapping
The action map $\mu$ in Equation 18 is the identity except for
$\mu(151643)=151645$ , mapping the student EOS identity to the teacher EOS identity.
The context conversion $\mathcal{T}$ leaves prompt tokens unchanged and applies this mapping
only to the first valid completion occurrence of 151643 in the teacher view. Padding, later
invalid positions, and stored student actions are unchanged. The valid sampled EOS action is
scored with its teacher identity. Teacher top- $k$ identities are canonicalized back to the
student identity before match and overlap features are computed. This convention aligns EOS
across model views without introducing a dual-EOS student stop set.
Reference-solution targets use the student EOS token 151643, which is also the sole generation stop token. The teacher-view mapping does not change this student target identity. The SFT, OPD + SFT, and MetaOPD + SFT controls use this same EOS convention.
B.6 Ablation and Resource Details
Variant definitions and SFT loss calibration.
Let $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}}^{w}$ denote the OPD loss on student-generated responses with token weights $w$ . In OPD + SFT, every valid token has weight one. In MetaOPD + SFT, the weighting network assigns the weights, which have mean one over the valid tokens in each response. Let $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}$ denote the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) loss: the mean token prediction loss on reference solutions under teacher forcing. It has the form of Equation 7, evaluated at the current student parameters rather than the virtual parameters. Each loss is averaged over the valid tokens in its own global batch. Both variants update the student with
|
| $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{student}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}}^{w}+\gamma\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}.$
|
The coefficient is calibrated once at the common Qwen3-1.7B-Base initialization using 16 disjoint pairs of 32-example OPD and reference-solution batches:
|
| $\gamma^{\star}=\operatorname{median}_{k=1}^{16}\frac{\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}+10^{-12}}.$
|
Thus $\gamma^{\star}$ approximately matches the two gradient scales at initialization; it is applied only to the SFT loss in the student objective, not to the MetaOPD outer objective.
The resulting coefficient is $\gamma^{\star}\approx 2.16$ ; the sweep uses half, equal, and double this value. Calibration uses training data only and is not selected by benchmark performance. Matching gradient norms does not imply that the two gradients have the same direction or induce equal optimizer updates.
For MetaOPD + SFT, the virtual inner objective remains weighted sampled OPD. The same global reference batch used for the outer objective supplies the SFT loss in the subsequent real student update; this loss is evaluated at the real student parameters.

Appendix C Motivating One-Step Comparison
C.1 Shared Setting
The comparison in Figure 1 uses Qwen3-1.7B-Base as the student and Qwen3-8B as the frozen teacher. Within each panel, all strategies start from the same initial student and optimizer state and perform one actual student update on the same student-generated responses. The optimizer starts with zero AdamW moments and completed-step count zero, uses a learning rate of $3\times 10^{-6}$ , and clips the global gradient norm at 1.0. Virtual and actual student updates use the same AdamW update rule, with the virtual update leaving the shared starting state unchanged. The completion mask retains the valid sampled EOS action.
We use four fixed prompt folds and three response-sampling seeds, giving 12 panels. Each panel contains 16 prompts with two responses per prompt. The seeds change only response sampling; the prompt selection and fold assignment remain fixed across seeds. We also sample two independent reference sets, A and B, from the MATH training pool, each containing 64 prompts and their reference solutions, and share both sets across all panels.
C.2 Compared Strategies
Uniform OPD assigns equal weights to valid response tokens. MetaOPD starts from a weighting network initialized to produce uniform weights. It uses reference set A to compute the validation loss after a virtual weighted OPD update, performs one meta-update of the network, and recomputes the token weights. These weights then determine the actual student update from the original student parameters and optimizer state. Reference set B evaluates the resulting student and does not enter weight learning.
EOPD (Jin et al., 2026) and TIP (Xu et al., 2026b) apply their supervision-allocation rules within the shared sampled-OPD framework. TIP uses the Soft-OR selection described in Appendix B.2.1. Random assigns random nonuniform token weights, providing a reference for allocation without validation-loss feedback. All candidate student updates use the shared responses, learning rate, and clipping threshold described above.
C.3 Evaluation and Aggregation
Let $\theta^{\prime}_{h}$ be the student after the actual update under strategy $h$ . We evaluate its teacher-forced reference-solution loss on set B, averaged over valid reference tokens, and report the paired reduction relative to Uniform OPD:
|
| $R_{h}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}(\theta^{\prime}_{\mathrm{Uniform}};B)-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}(\theta^{\prime}_{h};B).$
|
| (29)
Positive $R_{h}$ means that strategy $h$ yields a lower post-update validation loss than Uniform OPD, matching the direction in Figure 1(b). We average the paired reductions across the 12 panels and obtain 95% percentile confidence intervals from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of panels, retaining the pairing between strategies. These intervals are conditional on the fixed prompts and reference sets; the response-sampling seeds are not independent full training runs.
In this comparison, MetaOPD achieves the largest observed reduction, EOPD yields a smaller improvement, and TIP and Random remain close to Uniform OPD. The result supports using post-update performance to learn token weights in this one-step setting; it does not establish the effects of repeatedly applying the strategies over a full training run.

Appendix D gives the full protocol.
The checkpoint probes examine Qwen3-1.7B-Base trained with MetaOPD and the restricted-input variants of Section 4.3. Early, middle, and final denote MetaOPD steps 232, 464, and 696. The probes use each checkpoint’s student AdamW state, with global gradient-clipping threshold 1.0. All candidates use the same AdamW update rule as training at reference learning rate $3\times 10^{-6}$ , independent of the decayed training rate, and are not committed to the model.
For the checkpoint probes, student-generated responses provide the data for candidate OPD updates, while reference solutions are used to compute validation loss. Checkpoints share the same rollout prompts but generate responses separately. Each checkpoint’s panel contains 64 responses, divided into two groups of 32; candidate updates are computed separately for each group from that checkpoint. All candidate weighting rules and reference banks at a given checkpoint reuse these responses. We also use 256 separate prompt–reference-solution pairs, partitioned into 32 reference banks of eight examples each. The rollout prompts and reference examples are drawn from MATH test with MATH500 excluded. Within each reference bank, validation loss is averaged over valid reference-solution tokens.
We compute the raw $c_{i,t}$ at the weights produced by the checkpoint’s weighting network and define the centered diagnostic quantity
|
| $c^{\circ}_{i,t}=c_{i,t}-\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{s}m_{i,s}c_{i,s},$
|
| (30)
where $n_{i}=\sum_{s}m_{i,s}>0$ , using the ordinary mean over valid tokens within each response. The raw $c_{i,t}$ retains the derivative meaning in Equation 15; $c^{\circ}_{i,t}$ is a centered statistic, distinct from the weighted centering term in the derivative with respect to unnormalized weights. The diagnostic sign and magnitude comparisons below use $c^{\circ}_{i,t}$ and $\lvert c^{\circ}_{i,t}\rvert$ . Subtracting a response-wise constant does not change within-response Spearman correlations or ranks.
To assess consistency across reference batches, we combine the 32 banks into eight disjoint batches of 32 reference examples. For each batch, we average the vectors $\boldsymbol{d}$ from its four banks, weighted by their valid reference-token counts, then compute and center $c_{i,t}$ . We compare the resulting $c^{\circ}_{i,t}$ values for all pairs of batches within each response using Spearman correlation, sign agreement, and top-20% Jaccard overlap, then take the median over pairs and responses.
To assess learned-weight alignment, we average $\boldsymbol{d}$ across all 32 reference banks, weighted by their valid reference-token counts, using all 256 reference examples. We report the median within-response correlations of learned weights with the resulting $c^{\circ}_{i,t}$ values or their magnitudes. The 95% percentile intervals use 2,000 prompt-cluster bootstrap resamples, retaining all observations for each sampled prompt. This token-count weighting defines the derivative aggregation; the one-step loss comparisons below instead average banks equally.

D.3 One-Step Update Comparisons
We compare Uniform, Learned, and Reweighted allocations from the same checkpoint, computing candidate updates separately for each of the two rollout groups. Learned uses weights directly from the checkpoint’s frozen weighting network, without a new meta-update; this probe evaluates the allocation already learned at that checkpoint. Reweighted multiplies these learned weights by a linear factor in $[0.9,1.1]$ based on the within-response rank of $c^{\circ}_{i,t}$ , then restores unit response mean. It uses the same eight-example reference bank to compute the ranking and evaluate the update, so this comparison gives Reweighted access to its evaluation objective.
Let $B_{b}$ denote reference bank $b$ and $\theta^{\prime}_{h,b,g}$ the candidate for strategy $h$ , bank $b$ , and rollout group $g$ . Within each bank, we compute token-mean validation loss and average the two group results equally, then average the 32 banks equally:
|
| $\Delta L_{h}^{\mathrm{step}}=\frac{1}{32}\sum_{b=1}^{32}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{g=1}^{2}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}(\theta^{\prime}_{h,b,g};B_{b})-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}(\theta;B_{b})\right],$
|
| (31)
where $h\in\{\mathrm{Uniform},\mathrm{Learned},\mathrm{Reweighted}\}$ . Negative $\Delta L_{h}^{\mathrm{step}}$ indicates a lower mean loss than the unchanged checkpoint. Uniform and Learned do not use the bank to construct their weights, so their candidates depend only on the rollout group. The paired contrasts are
|
| $\displaystyle\Delta L_{\mathrm{Learned}-\mathrm{Uniform}}$
| $\displaystyle=\Delta L_{\mathrm{Learned}}^{\mathrm{step}}-\Delta L_{\mathrm{Uniform}}^{\mathrm{step}},$
|
| (32)
|
| $\displaystyle\Delta L_{\mathrm{Reweighted}-\mathrm{Learned}}$
| $\displaystyle=\Delta L_{\mathrm{Reweighted}}^{\mathrm{step}}-\Delta L_{\mathrm{Learned}}^{\mathrm{step}}.$
|
| (33)
The pre-update losses cancel because each contrast uses the same banks and rollout groups.
For Learned–Uniform (Figure 4(c); Table 12), we average the two paired group differences within each bank, then resample the 32 banks with replacement 10,000 times and recompute their equally weighted mean to obtain a 95% percentile interval. These intervals condition on the checkpoint and its fixed responses, excluding training-seed variation.
At the diagnostic reference learning rate of $3\times 10^{-6}$ , Learned yields lower validation loss than Uniform at all three checkpoints (Figures 4(c) and 12), with a final paired difference of approximately $-1.95\times 10^{-3}$ . Both allocations nevertheless increase mean loss relative to the unchanged checkpoint at every stage, so Learned’s advantage is relative rather than absolute.

These interventions use local derivative rankings to take finite steps at the reference rate; their relative ordering does not guarantee descent or improvement over the full training trajectory.

Appendix E Out-of-Domain Generalization
We evaluate the Qwen3-1.7B-Base checkpoints from the MATH main experiment on GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al., 2023), MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024), and AlpacaEval 2.0 (Dubois et al., 2024), without additional training on these tasks. Evaluation protocol.
Across these benchmarks, we use temperature 1.0, top- $p$ 0.8, and a maximum response length of 8192 tokens, matching the underlying decoding settings in Table 5. The number of responses and evaluation metric are benchmark-specific.
GPQA-Diamond contains 198 questions, for which we sample eight responses per question. Avg@8 measures average response accuracy, and Pass@8 measures the fraction of questions answered correctly at least once. MMLU-Pro contains 12,032 test questions and uses five-shot chain-of-thought demonstrations matched to the question’s category. We generate one response per question and report accuracy as Pass@1. AlpacaEval 2.0 compares model responses with GPT-4-Turbo reference responses using the weighted_alpaca_eval_gpt4_turbo annotator. We report both raw win rate (WR) and length-controlled win rate (LC-WR); these are preference-based scores, rather than answer-verification accuracy.

Results.
MetaOPD has the highest reported scores on both GPQA-Diamond metrics and MMLU-Pro Pass@1.
